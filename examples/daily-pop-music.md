# Daily pop music 001: Two kinds of forever

- **One composition:** Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan, “Mustt Mustt”
- **One aperture:** qawwali meeting Bristol dub, 1990–1992
- **Core time:** 7 minutes
- **Studio extension:** 8–12 optional minutes
- **Media:** 2 minutes maximum; official Real World Records pages

The seven-minute route is **Begin → Earn one name → Another machine of return →
Exit**. “Repair the map” and “Make a third architecture” are the optional studio.
The listening and no-audio routes reach the same central distinction.

## Begin with your hand, not a definition

Open one hand—or make four marks, taps, or imagined points.

Play the beginning of the official
[1992 live performance](https://realworldrecords.com/videos/mustt-mustt-live-at-womad-yokohama-1992/).
Stop at the first unmistakable return of the central vocal phrase, or at 1:15,
whichever comes first.

Each time something recognizable returns, fold one finger, add one mark, or
register one point. Do not count beats. Notice what the return *does*.

Choose a shape before reading on:

- a circle—back where it began;
- a staircase—the same place, higher;
- a magnet—everything pulled toward the return;
- a wave—return made from continuous motion;
- another shape you can draw in five seconds.

There is no personality result hidden in the choice. It is a claim about this
performance, and the next listen can overturn it.

## Now earn one name

Qawwali is a living Sufi devotional and professional musical practice associated
especially with present-day Pakistan and north India. In the performance, a
lead vocal act, answering voices, recurring phrase, clapping, percussion, and
harmonium do not make repetition into stasis. Return gives performers and
listeners something to recognize, answer, stretch, and intensify.

That description is a doorway, not a definition of every qawwali setting. A
festival video is not the same social event as a devotional gathering, and this
lesson will not detach or improvise a translation of sacred text for effect.

Look at your chosen shape. Keep it, revise it, or cross it out.

## Put another machine of return around it

Now play only the first 45 seconds available from Real World Records' official
page for the
[Massive Attack remix](https://realworldrecords.com/releases/mustt-mustt-massive-attack-remix/).

Use the other hand this time. Fold a finger when the voice returns; open one
when bass, pulse, delay, or empty space changes how the return lands.

Which claim survives?

1. The remix replaces repetition with forward motion.
2. The remix makes an old vocal practice modern.
3. The remix places one architecture of repetition inside another.
4. None of these; write a better sentence.

Choose before continuing. If you want the direct answer now: **3 is the
strongest starting model**, though 4 may beat it if it names audible evidence.

Here is the hinge of the lesson:

> The remix did not bring repetition to qawwali. It made two technologies of
> return audible against each other.

“Technology” here includes trained bodies, responsive ensemble knowledge,
memory, microphones, mixing, bass, delay, and the studio—not only electronics.
That is an interpretation of the encounter, not a claim that qawwali and dub
share one origin.

## Studio extension: repair the map

A lazy map draws this:

`timeless tradition → Western studio → modern pop`

Cross it out.

Try this one, still incomplete:

`qawwali practice and recording ⇄ Nusrat + Michael Brook at Real World ⇄ Bristol dub listening and Massive Attack's remix`

The double arrows matter. Everyone arrives with technique, history, and
judgment. Real World Records calls the remix a convergence of Sufi qawwali
singing and Bristol dub; the market label “world music” can nevertheless tempt
a listener to imagine that only Bristol occupies the present.

Add one word over each arrow. Choose from `collaboration`, `translation`,
`framing`, `commerce`, `hospitality`, `extraction`, `mishearing`, or your own.
You may use different words in opposite directions.

## Studio extension: make a third architecture

Write four cells. Put `X` where a phrase returns and `·` where you leave space.

`[ ] [ ] [ ] [ ]`

Run the four cells three times, but alter exactly one relation on each return:
volume, spacing, response, density, or who acts. Tap it, gesture it, or simply
mark it on the page.

Now finish this sentence:

> My return becomes an intensification rather than a reset when __________.

That sentence is your theory. Test it against the live performance, the remix,
or another recurring chorus you meet today.

## Exit with one changed question

Without looking back, retrieve the correction:

> The remix did not modernize qawwali by adding repetition; it __________.

A strong answer is: **placed one already-developed practice of return inside
another and changed the relations around the returning voice.** Your wording
may be better if it is more faithful to what you noticed.

Next time a song repeats, replace “Is this repetitive?” with:

> **What changed because this returned?**

That question travels—to a chorus, a dance loop, a prayer, a sample, a joke, a
ritual, or a habit—without pretending those things are equivalent.

You are done.

## Equivalent no-audio route

Use two structural cards:

1. **Live card:** lead phrase → answering voices → recurring phrase → greater
   density or intensity; harmonium, clapping, and percussion support the social
   return.
2. **Remix card:** recurring vocal → bass and pulse → delay or negative space →
   vocal return heard inside a newly mixed field.

Draw each card as a shape and retrieve the central correction: one architecture
of return was placed inside another. That completes the seven-minute core. The
map repair and four-cell third architecture remain optional studio extensions,
exactly as they are in the audio route. This preserves the lesson's structural
and historical work; it does not pretend to reproduce vocal timbre, embodied
performance, or devotional affect.
